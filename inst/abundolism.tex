\subsection{Abstract}\label{abstract}

Why do some lineages diversify while others do not? This remains a
central question in evolutionary ecology. A long-standing assumption,
dating to Darwin and embedded in the Unified Neutral Theory of
Biodiversity, holds that abundant species should speciate at higher
rates. Conversely, theoretical and empirical work highlights the
possibility that rare and dispersal-limited clades might be more prone
to speciation. Using a birth-death-immigration model with protracted
speciation in a multi-population landscape connected by limited
dispersal, we show that abundance has a hump-shaped effect on
probability of speciation. Our model reveals that intermediate abundance
maximizes speciation probability because larger populations disperse
more, swamping regional differentiation and inhibiting speciation
completion, while smaller populations lack the persistence and incipient
speciation needed to diversify. We find empirical support for this
prediction with an analysis of data from arthropods endemic to Hawai`i,
where genus-level species richness shows a significant hump-shaped
relationship with mean genus abundance. These findings provide a
mechanistic explanation for a nuanced relationship between abundance and
diversification.

\subsection{Introduction}\label{introduction}

A fundamental question in evolutionary ecology concerns why some clades
are diverse while others are not. Are there properties of species and
lineages that promote speciation? One line of inquiry with a long and
convoluted history is the role of commonness and rarity in the process
of diversification. Going back over 150 years, Darwin (1859) argued that
widespread, abundant species should be superior competitors and thus
more likely to give rise to new species.

Building on this foundation, Yule's (1925) model, which became a
landmark contribution to modeling diversification, was initially
intended as a way to incorporate demography into predictions for
diversification and clade richness (Pennell and MacPherson 2025).
Despite these deep roots in evolutionary thinking, little consensus has
emerged on the relationship between abundance and diversification.
Darwin's line of thinking carried forward to macroecologists who
attempted to model, with some empirical support, the presumed positive
association between commonness, fitness, and speciation (Maurer 1999).

In parallel, paleontologists searched for relationships between
commonness and diversification, alternately finding evidence for a
positive relationship (Krug et al. 2008) but also a negative
relationship (Stanley 1986, Jablonski and Roy 2003). Empirical studies
of modern taxa have largely found that commonness is negatively or
inconclusively related to measures of diversification (Jablonski and Roy
2003, Makarieva and Gorshkov 2004, Greenberg and Mooers 2017, Smyčka et
al. 2023, Afonso Silva et al. 2025, but see Hay et al. 2022). While some
of these studies focus specifically on average abundance, others on
range size, and some on both, the two macroecological properties are
most often strongly correlated, a fascinating macroecology pattern in
and of itself (Brown 1995, Gaston 2003). This correlation may be caused,
at least partly, by the net increase in dispersal of larger populations
owing simply to chance: if each individual carries a probability of
dispersing, more individuals in a population will lead to more overall
dispersal (Hubbell 2001). It is also of course possible that shared
underlying mechanisms cause both increased population growth rate and
increased dispersal rate (Gaston et al. 1997, Holt et al. 1997).

More recent theoretical and modeling work has largely assumed a positive
relationship between commonness and diversification. Hubbell (2001)
perhaps started this trend with the inclusion of ``point mutation''
speciation in the Unified Neutral Theory of Biodiversity (UNTB). Because
point mutation speciation assumes a constant probability of speciation
for every individual, lineages with more individuals will experience
more speciation events in the UNTB (Hubbell 2001, Etienne et al. 2007).
The speciation mechanism first proposed by Hubbell (2001) was never an
accurate model of real diversification (Ricklefs 2003), but even later
attempts to increase the realism of speciation in the UNTB retain the
emergent property that more abundant lineages will undergo more
speciation. Protracted speciation (Rosindell et al. 2010) again assumes
that incipient speciation is constant across individuals and thus full
speciation will scale positively with lineage abundance; fission
speciation (Etienne and Haegeman 2011) also assumes the probability of
fission (the event that leads to speciation) increases with the number
of individuals in a species. Largely independently from UNTB and its
descendants, phylogenetic models of geographic change and
diversification also explicitly assume that more widespread species have
greater opportunity for speciation (Goldberg et al. 2011).

The process that underlies speciation itself may suggest something
different in terms of the relationship between speciation, commonness,
and rarity: dispersal. Dispersal is a key mechanism by which populations
can become isolated or connected, potentially leading to speciation or
admixture (Yamaguchi 2022). Dispersal figures prominently in
phylogenetic models of the geographic range and diversification (Matzke
2014), it is necessary to maintain local biodiversity in the UNTB
(Hubbell 2001), and it is the mechanism connecting abundance to range
size in both neutral (Hubbell 2001) and non-neutral (Brown 1995)
macroecological models.

Due to the potential for dispersal to both create population isolates
but also swamp out regional differences that could have led to
speciation, the connection between dispersal ability as a biological
trait and speciation as an evolutionary outcome remains a fertile line
of inquiry. Some empirical studies have found that measures of
diversification have a hump-shaped or negative relationship with
morphological proxies for dispersal ability (Price and Wagner 2004,
Claramunt et al. 2012, Czekanski-Moir and Rundell 2019) while others
have found a positive to flat relationship between dispersal ability and
diversification (Claramunt et al. 2025). Agent-based model simulations
have supported the idea of a trade-off between too little dispersal
leading to population instability and too much dispersal leading to
admixture with intermediate dispersal balancing the two extremes
allowing for speciation (Birand et al. 2012, Ashby et al. 2020, Ciccheto
et al. 2024).

But dispersal is not just a trait connected to morphology or imposed in
a simulation, it emerges from population dynamics: each individual
carries some per-capita probability of dispersing, but because that
probability is applied across every individual in the population, the
total dispersal rate (the total number of dispersal events per unit
time) scales linearly with abundance, thus species with more individuals
produce more dispersal overall. This is the assumption, a realistic one,
in birth-death-immigration models (Kendall 1948). Therefore, if
commonness and dispersal affect speciation, their effects are
interconnected because population-level dispersal rate increases both
with intrinsic per-capita dispersal probability and with abundance. It
also remains an open debate whether their effects result from the
determinism of adaptive evolution as Darwin (1859) believed or instead
emerge from chance.

Here we develop a birth-death-immigration model with protracted
speciation embedded in a landscape of multiple local populations
connected with limited dispersal to investigate the role of abundance
and stochastic dispersal in modulating the probability of speciation.
Contrary to Darwin (1859) and UNTB (Hubbell 2001, Etienne et al. 2007,
Rosindell et al. 2010, Etienne and Haegeman 2011), we find that
intermediate abundances lead to the greatest probability for speciation.
Critically, this result does not depend in any way on whether
populations are more or less adaptively fit, it only depends on a
balance between large enough abundances to accrue sufficient probability
of incipient speciation but small enough abundances to not lose regional
differentiation due to increased dispersal. We also analyze real data on
the species richness and abundance of arthropod communities in Hawai`i
finding empirical evidence for intermediate abundance promoting
speciation. The isolation of Hawai`i, leading to tractable endemic
radiations, and its known geologic age, ranging from still forming to
4--6 million years, make it an ideal study system for investigating the
interplay of abundance and speciation.

\subsection{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsubsection{Simulating a birth-death-immigration process with
protracted
speciation}\label{simulating-a-birth-death-immigration-process-with-protracted-speciation}

We simulate a birth-death-immigration process with protracted speciation
in a modified metapopulation (Hanski 1998) setting. For simplicity, we
consider only a single species (not multi-species) metapopulation.
Constraining our simulations to a single species allows us to focus on
the processes of interest---population dynamics and speciation---without
entering into debates on multi-species dynamics (HilleRisLambers et al.
2012, Rosindell et al. 2012).

In this simulation framework there are a number of local populations
(minimum two are needed, we simulate 10) fully connected by local
dispersal as well as a global source population connected to each local
population by dispersal. The global source population is considered
extremely large relative to local populations such that extinction of
the global source population is unlikely on the time scale of local
dynamics (we approximate this assumption by never allowing the source
population to go extinct). Local populations start at size 0 and grow
and shrink according to local births and deaths, dispersal between local
populations, and dispersal from the source pool.

Our simulation model is consistent with other simulation models derived
from the UNTB (Hubbell 2001, Rosindell and Harmon 2013, Rosindell et al.
2015), but unlike the multi-species perspective of those models, we
concern ourselves only with a single species and whether that one
species undergoes a speciation event. In this way we do not make the
assumption of per capita neutrality across species, meaning our results
could generalize to multi-species eco-evolutionary models based on niche
theories (e.g. Tilman 1982, Chesson 2000, Letten et al. 2017). We also
relax the zero-sum and fixed time step approaches of most UNTB-like
models. Instead, we initialize all patches as empty and let their
numbers of individuals grow and shrink with birth, death, and dispersal.
We also use a Gillespie algorithm (Gillespie 1976, 1977) to simulate
dynamics. In the Gillespie algorithm each discrete time step is an event
chosen at random in proportion to the rate of occurrence of that event
(i.e.~not the fixed death-replacement cycle of the UNTB; Hubbell
(2001)). Although events are sampled at discrete time steps, time itself
is measured in actual units of time, not time steps, by sampling sojourn
times between events from an exponential distribution with rate equal to
the sum of all rates for all possible events.

\paragraph{Protracted speciation}\label{protracted-speciation}

In our simulation model a new species arises via a modified protracted
speciation process (Rosindell et al. 2010) similar to the model of Tao
et al. (2021). Protracted speciation was first proposed by Rosindell et
al. (2010) to try to bring more realism to the speciation process of the
UNTB which originally modeled speciation as a point mutation-like
process in which a single individual gets randomly flipped to a new
species identity with some small probability \(\nu\) (Hubbell 2001).
Protracted speciation instead assumes that this point mutation-like
process, again with probability \(\nu\), only flips an individual to be
an \emph{incipient} species; if the lineage of that incipient species
survives \(\tau\) time steps, the process is said to reach full
speciation but if the lineage dies out before \(\tau\) time steps, no
new species is formed (Rosindell et al. 2010).

We extend the protracted speciation model to a multi-patch simulation
landscape. In our model, after a population is founded through
immigration it, the newly founded population, has a small probability of
becoming an incipient new species. If an incipient speciation event
occurs, and in the absence of subsequent immigration, the population of
the incipient new species must persist \(\tau\) time units for full
speciation. If the population does not persist for \(\tau\) time units,
the incipient new species is lost without ever becoming a fully new
species.

After an incipient speciation event, subsequent immigration events slow
the progress toward full speciation. We model this by adding a small
increment to \(\tau\) equal to \(\xi / n_i\) where \(\xi\) is a
parameter we can set and \(n_i\) is the size of the population
containing the incipient species. Due to \(n_i\) in the denominator,
this slowdown will be felt less by large populations and more by smaller
populations in agreement with population genetic theory on the effect of
immigration on population differentiation (Wright 1931, Mills and
Allendorf 1996).

Tao et al. (2021) also developed a similar multi-patch protracted
speciation model, but ours is different in three ways:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  Tao et al. (2021) assume every population founded by immigration is an
  incipient species; we assume that a new population founded through
  immigration remains part of the parent species unless an explicit
  incipient speciation event occurs.
\item
  In our Gillespie algorithm approach, time to full speciation is
  measured in actual time units, not discrete time steps.
\item
  Tao et al. (2021) assume that a fixed constant is added to \(\tau\)
  for every immigration event
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Simulation rules}\label{simulation-rules}

The preceding sections explained our simulation model; here we summarize
the rules by which the simulation progresses:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  All local populations are initialized as empty, to be filled by
  immigration.
\item
  Per capita birth, death, immigration, and incipient speciation all
  happen independently and are determined by their own respective rates.
\item
  Immigration is modeled as the dispersal of a newly born individual,
  thus the population from which the individual emigrates is unchanged;
  this birth-then-emigrate mechanism is a widely used simplification in
  models inspired by the UNTB (Hubbell 2001, Rosindell and Harmon 2013,
  Rosindell et al. 2015, Tao et al. 2021). Each individual carries a per
  capita rate of producing a dispersal event, thus emigration from a
  patch scales with the population size of that patch, but dispersal
  from the source pool is not density dependent. The recipient
  population of an immigration event is chosen uniformly at random, the
  population size of the receiving population does not impact
  probability of receiving an immigration event.
\item
  Speciation has 2 steps:

  \begin{enumerate}
  \def\labelenumii{\roman{enumii}.}
  \tightlist
  \item
    After a population is founded through immigration, incipient
    speciation can change that population to an incipient new species
    with a per capita rate of \(\nu\). Subsequent incipient speciation
    events in the same local population are ignored because the
    population is already an incipient new species. Multiple incipient
    species can occur across different patches, but not within the same
    patch.
  \item
    If an incipient species survives long enough it becomes a new
    species; ``long enough'' is determined by a waiting time parameter
    \(\tau\)---the larger \(\tau\), the longer the time frame for a
    fully new species to emerge.
  \end{enumerate}
\item
  Immigration between local communities and from the global source pool
  slows the progress toward full speciation; if an incipient species has
  to wait \(\tau\) time units (in the absence of immigration) until it
  is a full species, each immigration event adds an increment to
  \(\tau\) of \(\xi / n_i\).
\item
  If an individual of an incipient species disperses to a different
  population, it is subsumed within that population and its incipient
  status is lost.
\item
  If a local population goes extinct, the speciation tracking for that
  patch is reset.
\item
  The simulation is stopped under one of two conditions: i) if any
  incipient species becomes a fully diverged new species, or ii) if the
  simulation reaches the maximum designated number of iterations.
\end{enumerate}

Because of the stopping conditions, each simulation replicate will
either yield one and only one full speciation, or no full speciation
events at all.

\subsubsection{Simulation experiment}\label{simulation-experiment}

Table~\ref{tbl-pars} summarizes the simulation parameters in our model
and the range of values we use in our simulation experiment. In all
cases parameter values are drawn from a uniform distribution. A total of
5,000 simulations were run, each with a unique set of randomly sampled
parameters. Parameter values and the number of iterations were chosen
with three considerations in mind:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  speciation should be possible to observe in a reasonable number of
  simulation iterations for computational practicality
\item
  abundances should be within realistic ranges
\item
  timescales should be within reasonable bounds
\end{enumerate}

In the context of the speciation-related parameter values we used,
timescales should be interpreted in units of 10,000 years. Thus the
maximum value of \(\tau\) is 2,000,000 years and the maximum rate of
incipient speciation \(\nu\) is 0.1 per individual per 10,000 years,
leading to rates of new species divergence roughly in line with
published estimates (Hedges et al. 2015).

\paragraph{Analysis of simulation experiment
results}\label{analysis-of-simulation-experiment-results}

Our primary focus is on the relationship between abundance and
probability of speciation. We do this by comparing the presence or
absence of a full speciation event to species abundance averaged over
time and patches. However, we must account for the stopping conditions
of our simulation being dependent on the very
process---speciation---that we are analyzing. Simulation runs reaching
full speciation will have fewer time steps than those reaching the
pre-determined time step limit. We account for this by cutting each
simulation run into a set of time step windows of fixed width
(i.e.~fixed number of time steps). We then analyze the presence or
absence of speciation and average abundance within these non-overlapping
windows of fixed width. A window is marked as containing a speciation
event or not. We calculate abundance as the average population size
across time steps of the entire metapopulation. We apply this approach
to the analysis of speciation versus abundance, but also to auxiliary
analyses of how speciation and abundance respond to model parameters.
Those auxiliary analyses include visualizing average abundance across
different birth and death rates and dispersal rates, as well as fitting
models of the probability of speciation as a function of model
parameters. Because choice of window size could affect analysis results,
we re-run the analysis of abundance versus speciation using windows of
different width.

In fitting any models with these simulation results cut into fixed-width
windows, we must account for the non-independence between windows from
the same model run. We do this using generalized linear mixed effects
models {[}using R package \emph{glmmTMB}; McGillycuddy et al. (2025){]}
where simulation run is a random effect. We report confidence intervals
rather than hypothesis tests because sample sizes from simulations are
arbitrary and thus degrees of freedom can be manipulated to produce
statistically significant results from biologically meaningless
patterns. By contrast, confidence intervals more meaningfully indicate
the magnitude of effects in the simulation experiment.

\subsubsection{Analysis of arthropod richness and abundance from
Hawai`i}\label{analysis-of-arthropod-richness-and-abundance-from-hawaii}

To bring real data to bear on our insights from simulations, we analyze
the species richness and average per-species abundance of arthropod
genera in Hawai`i. Endemic arthropods in Hawai`i are a uniquely ideal
study system because, although only an approximation, most arthropod
genera only arrived in Hawai`i once, meaning all endemic species of
those genera represent \emph{in situ} diversification (Cowie and Holland
2008). For the purpose of our analysis, we proceed with this
approximation. The unique situation in Hawai`i, that each arthropod
genus arrived approximately only once on this geologically young
archipelago, also avoids the problem that taxonomic ranks are temporally
inconsistent at a global level (Avise and Liu 2011).

To calculate species richness per genus we use the Bernice Pauahi Bishop
Museum's checklist of arthropods in Hawai`i (Nishida 2002). This
checklist represents the current understanding of terrestrial arthropod
species richness in Hawai`i, though is inevitably incomplete for a place
supporting rapid diversification. To estimate average per-species
abundance for each genus we use an extensive survey of arthropods from
across the Hawaiian Archipelago (Gruner 2007). The survey data from
Gruner (2007) comprise sample locations from 45 trees across 5 sites.
Sites are located on Hawai`i Island, Moloka`i, and Kaua`i and follow the
Long Substrate Age Gradient plots established by Vitousek et al. (1995)
that are constrained to be within similar montane rainforest
environments.

Combining the checklist with survey data resulted in the retention of
118 endemic genera out of 597 total from the checklist, representing a
loss of 80.23\%. While this is a large percentage, it is also not
unexpected. The survey data constitute a very small sample of the
geographic space of the Hawaiian Archipelago as well as a small sample
of the extremely heterogeneous environmental space of the archipelago.
\ssec{sec-miss-gen} evaluates the impact of these missing genera on our
analyses, finding that our results are robust to this subsampling of
total genera.

Average per-species abundance was estimated for each genus as the mean
of each species's abundance for all species belonging to that genus
across all sample locations. We use average per-species abundance to
represent the measure of commonness or rarity of a genus because
speciation occurs at the species level, or lower, so abundance of higher
taxonomic groups is not as relevant. We can also estimate the average
abundance of a species with lower error than the total abundance of a
genus. In \ssec{sec-tot-abund} we further study the choice of using
average per-species abundance and find it to be a rigorous one.

We are interested in determining if the number of species per genus (a
proxy for diversification) peaks at intermediate per-species abundances.
To do this we fit a quadratic generalized linear model with species
richness as response and average per-species abundance as explanatory
variable. But the question remains, what probability distribution should
we assume for number of species? Kendall (1948) showed that a
birth-death process will result in a geometric distribution of
descendants. Conditional on lineage survival at time \(t\) after initial
establishment, we can parameterize the geometric probability mass
function as

\begin{equation}
f(y) = (1 - \psi(t))^{y-1}\psi(t)
\end{equation}

where \(y\) is the number of species in a lineage. \(\psi(t)\) is the
probability that a lineage survives but we do not observe any
bifurcation events by time \(t\). Thus \(1 - \psi(t)\) is the
probability of the complement which is observing at least one
bifurcation event leaving multiple descendants at time \(t\). We will
call \(1 - \psi(t)\) the ``observable diversification probability.''
\(\psi(t)\) depends on rates of speciation and extinction in addition to
time \(t\).

The number of species in any given lineage \(i\) extant at time \(t\)
will follow a geometric distribution with probability parameter
\(p_i = \psi_i(t_i)\). But we do not know \(p_i\) because we do not know
the arrival times of most arthropod lineages in Hawai`i---thus we do not
know \(t_i\)---and we do not know their rates of speciation and
extinction. However, we can model our uncertainty in \(p_i\) by letting
it be a random variable drawn from a beta distribution. Allowing \(p_i\)
to be a random variable helps statistically account for the confounding
effects of variable diversification rate and lineage age on lineage
richness (McPeek and Brown 2007, Scholl and Wiens 2016, Yu and Wiens
2024). Drawing \(p_i\) from a beta distribution averages over these
possible confounding effects.

The resulting beta-geometric distribution has parameters \(\alpha\) and
\(\beta\):

\begin{equation}
P(y; \alpha, \beta) = \frac{B(\alpha + 1, y + \beta - 1)}{B(\alpha, \beta)}
\end{equation}

where \(B\) is the beta function.

Interestingly enough, the Yule-Simons distribution, historically
developed to model number of species in genera (Yule 1925), is a special
case of the beta-geometric distribution when \(\beta = 1\) (Irwin 1975).
In our beta-geometric model we will not fix \(\beta = 1\) but instead
allow it to be a free parameter, thus not making the strict birth-only
assumption of Yule (1925).

Our final, quadratic model relating genus richness to average
per-species abundance in a genus is

\begin{align}
y_i &\sim \text{BetaGeometric}(\alpha_i, \beta) \\
\log(\alpha_i) &= a_0 + a_1 x_i + a_2 x_i^2 \label{eq-link} \\
\log(\beta) &= b_0
\end{align}

where \(x_i\) is average per-species abundance of lineage \(i\). This
model means that we allow the \(\alpha\) parameter of the beta-geometric
distribution to change with average per-species abundance but we assume
the \(\beta\) parameter is fixed across lineages. A fixed \(\beta\)
across lineages still allows sufficient flexibility of the model without
introducing additional parameters for which there may be insufficient
power to reliably estimate. From this model our estimate for the
observable diversification probability for lineage \(i\) is
\(1 - \hat{p_i} = 1 - \frac{\alpha_i}{\alpha_i + \beta}\).

To evaluate the significance of an intermediate peak in diversification
across average per-species abundances (i.e.~the quadratic term) we also
fit a model without quadratic terms:

\begin{align}
y_i &\sim \text{BetaGeometric}(\alpha_i, \beta) \\
\log(\alpha_i) &= a_0 + a_1 x_i \\
\log(\beta) &= b_0
\end{align}

We then compare quadratic and linear models with a likelihood ratio test
to evaluate significance of the intermediate peak.

While the \emph{VGAM} package (Yee 2025) in R enables beta-geometric
generalized linear modeling, its implementation is based on iterative
Fisher scoring which is unnecessary and computationally slow for our
application. Therefore, we use functionality from \emph{VGAM} to write a
custom likelihood maximization routine that we use to estimate model
parameters. More details on this approach are provided in
\ssec{sec-bgeom}.

\subsection{Discussion}\label{discussion}

Taken all together these results indicate support, from both theoretical
and empirical perspectives, for the idea that intermediate abundances
promote speciation. Using a theoretical simulation-based model we show
that the chance of speciation peaks at intermediate abundances and that
this peak is robust even when considering the interactive effects other
model parameters might have on speciation. This result is consistent
with the idea that these intermediate abundances represent a balance
between dispersal, which slows speciation, and population persistence
plus per capita incipient speciation, which are necessary for
speciation. The balance would arise because total dispersal is lowest at
low abundance (meaning the rate of transition from incipient to full
speciation is greatest at low abundance) while persistence and incipient
speciation are highest at high abundance. Our work suggests an
intriguing possibility that if properties like abundance and dispersal
are emergent species-level traits that modulate speciation, they could
be acted upon by macroevolutionary selection (Rabosky and McCune 2010).

Our results are novel in that they go against the widespread assumption
that more abundant lineages should be those that produce more new
speciation---an assumption tracing back to Darwin (1859) and also
integral to the unified neutral theory of biodiversity (Hubbell 2001).
However, our theoretical results are also grounded in published
hypotheses, specifically the role of total dispersal in modulating
speciation (Price and Wagner 2004, Claramunt et al. 2012, Czekanski-Moir
and Rundell 2019, Yamaguchi 2022).

We arrive at these results by building on established modeling
frameworks (Hubbell 2001, Rosindell et al. 2010), but we explore how
abundance drives total dispersal, in turn swamping regional
differentiation (Price and Wagner 2004, Birand et al. 2012, Claramunt et
al. 2012, Czekanski-Moir and Rundell 2019, Ashby et al. 2020, Ciccheto
et al. 2024). The model produces realistic patterns of abundance as
responding to growth rate and, when growth rate is low, responding to
dispersal via a rescue-effect like mechanism (Brown and Kodric-Brown
1977). The scaling of abundance and total dispersal is driven in large
part by abundance (because abundances can reach large sizes) such that
after accounting for abundance there is no residual effect on speciation
probability of per capita dispersal, or of the base-level waiting time
to full speciation in the absence of immigration. However, the GLMM
including per capita dispersal, despite a seemingly non-significant
dispersal term, does shift location of the peak probability of
speciation. The shift is toward larger abundance. This would make sense
if greater per capita dispersal slows (but does not prevent) eventual
full speciation and the longer wait time to full speciation allows
populations to reach a higher abundance.

The fact that greater incipient speciation rate increases the chance of
full speciation is not surprising (more incipient events yield more
random trials for possible full speciation). Similarly, the shift in
peak speciation toward lower abundance with greater incipient speciation
rate is likely due to a shorter total wait time to full speciation
because incipient speciation happens more quickly. It might seem
counter-intuitive that greater growth rate would decrease the abundance
at which speciation peaks, but when growth rates are greater, then
abundance is gained locally (increasing regional differentiation)
without the need for immigration to buoy it; thus if full speciation is
reach, it will be at lower abundances.

Our empirical results are qualitatively consistent with the theoretical
finding that speciation peaks at intermediate abundances. The empirical
results are made possible by the unique evolutionary setting of the
Hawaiian Archipelago, where extreme isolation aids in the direct
observation of diversification (Wagner and Funk 1995, Price and Wagner
2004). However, inferring past diversification from modern-only data has
been shown to have multiple technical pitfalls including artifacts of
sampling (Gruner et al. 2008), model non-identifiability (Louca and
Pennell 2020), and difficulty in disambiguating effects of clade age and
clade diversification rate (Yu and Wiens 2024). We test for sampling
artifacts due to possibly under-sampling rare taxa (\ssec{sec-miss-gen})
and due to choice of summary statistic (\ssec{sec-tot-abund}) and find
our results are robust to these checks.

The empirical and theoretical results are also distinct and cannot be
interpreted as quantitatively equivalent. While both produce a
hump-shaped relationship between abundance and measures of speciation or
diversification, this does not mean that the simulation model parameters
have a one-to-one mapping onto the parameters of a hypothetical
diversification model that could have produced the empirical patterns.
The parameters of the simulation model have to be interpreted separately
from the empirical results because of key differences between the
simulation set-up and the empirical analysis. Firstly, the ``response
variables'' of each analysis are different---the simulation is able to
directly observe speciation and thus we directly analyze speciation
probability; conversely, we cannot directly observe speciation in the
empirical system and so instead model \(1 - \hat{p}\), the ``observable
diversification probability.'' The fact that we stop a simulation run
once speciation is achieved also makes the simulated results different
from the empirical system---in the real world, time does not stop if
speciation occurs; instead, some clades continue to diversify and some
go extinct. Our simulation model is thus one of the probability of first
speciation and does not account for how the probability of speciation
may itself change over the course of diversification (e.g., Rabosky
2009, Wagner et al. 2014, Calcagno et al. 2017, Foote 2023). The
continued diversification of real world clades could also explain the
flatter relationship of \(1 - \hat{p}\) across abundances. Additionally,
while both the simulation model and empirical analysis are concerned
with abundance, the way abundance is recorded in each is different. In
the simulation we know the complete abundance of every local population
and we scale rates such that local abundances are computationally
tractable. Real world abundances (perhaps up to \(10^8\)) would not be
computationally manageable. Also unlike the simulations, the real world
data represent tiny subsamples of the whole populations.

Nonetheless, the simulation model provides a proof-of-concept and a
mechanism for how speciation might depend on abundance in a more nuanced
way than previously considered. Critically, our model does not depend on
a neutrality assumption to arrive at this conclusion. Although our model
is inspired by the agent-based modeling approaches of neutral theories
(Hubbell 2001, Rosindell and Harmon 2013, Rosindell et al. 2015) we only
model populations of a single species and thus make no assumption about
how multiple species interact or whether different species have
different eco-evolutionary rates. Thus our findings apply regardless of
whether one assumes neutral- or niche-based dynamics. We also do not
assume density dependence, as this is not our focus. However, one can
postulate that positive density-dependent dispersal (Travis et al.
1999), which causes total dispersal rate to increase super-linearly with
abundance, could further accentuate our findings. Conversely, negative
density-dependent dispersal (Rodrigues and Johnstone 2014) could dampen
the effects we find, but, so long as per-capita dispersal remains a
non-zero probability, likely not eliminate them.

Because our model focuses on a single species and thus makes no
assumptions about dynamics or interactions between species we highlight
the unnecessary assumptions of previous approaches. Darwin (1859), for
example, was also interested in the relationship between abundance and
speciation. He assumed that more abundant species gave rise to more new
species because those abundant species were biologically dominant to
less abundant one, and that this dominance promoted both abundance and
diversification (Darwin 1859, Maurer 1999). The language of Darwin
(1859), including reference to ``conquer{[}ing{]}'' {[}p.~125{]} and
``triumphant'' {[}p.~126{]} clades, cannot be separated from discourse
then and now that seeks to justify racism and colonial violence
(Brantlinger 2003, Hesse 2007, Fuentes 2021, Diogo et al. 2023). Beyond
the inherent amorality of such assumptions, our work shows they are
unnecessary in explaining why abundance connects to speciation. We also
show that these unnecessary assumptions lead to a prediction---a
positive relationship between abundance and speciation---that may not
universally hold.
